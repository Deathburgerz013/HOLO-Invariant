The Beginning of ReligionReligion emerged in prehistory through symbolic and ritual behaviors among early humans and possibly earlier hominins. The oldest credible archaeological evidence appears in the Middle Paleolithic period (approximately 300,000–50,000 years ago) in the form of intentional burials of the dead. These suggest beliefs in an afterlife, spiritual continuation, or ritual respect for the deceased. 

en.wikipedia.org

Key validated examples include:Burials associated with early Homo sapiens at Qafzeh and Skhul caves in the Levant (~90,000–120,000 years ago, with some contexts extending toward ~130,000 ya), frequently accompanied by grave goods such as red ochre, perforated marine shells, stone tools, and animal bones, pointing to deliberate symbolic treatment of the dead.
Neanderthal burials at sites such as Shanidar Cave in Iraq (~60,000–80,000 years ago). Intentional burial is widely accepted, but specific claims of associated floral offerings (the “flower burial” interpretation of pollen around Shanidar 4) remain debated, as later analyses suggest the pollen could result from natural processes, rodent activity, or post-depositional factors rather than deliberate ritual placement. 

en.wikipedia.org

Phylogenetic reconstruction of religious traits across modern hunter-gatherer societies identifies animism—the belief that spirits or souls inhabit natural objects, animals, plants, and phenomena—as the earliest and most fundamental trait, present in the common ancestor of these groups. This precedes beliefs in an afterlife, shamanism, or ancestor worship in the evolutionary sequence. 

pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

Stronger, more unambiguous evidence appears in the Upper Paleolithic (~50,000–12,000 years ago) with symbolic artifacts such as Venus figurines (e.g., Venus of Hohle Fels ~35,000–40,000 ya; Venus of Willendorf ~25,000–30,000 ya), widely interpreted as linked to fertility, femininity, ritual practice, or spiritual reverence (though exact meanings remain speculative and may also include self-representation or protective functions), and cave art (e.g., Chauvet Cave ~36,000 ya) that may depict ritual scenes, animal spirits, or trance-related experiences consistent with early shamanistic practices involving altered states of consciousness and mediation between human and spirit realms. 

en.wikipedia.org

A key transitional development occurs at Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Anatolia, Turkey (~9600–8000 BCE). This Pre-Pottery Neolithic monumental complex of circular enclosures with massive T-shaped pillars carved with wild animals, snakes, and abstract symbols was constructed by pre-agricultural hunter-gatherers. It is interpreted as a major ritual or ceremonial center for communal gatherings, feasts, possible ancestor veneration, or spirit/animal worship, demonstrating organized symbolic and social capacities for religious activity well before the rise of farming or permanent villages. 

biblicalarchaeology.org

No written religious records exist until the invention of writing around 3200 BCE in Mesopotamia (cuneiform) and Egypt (hieroglyphs), marking the shift from prehistoric to historic religions.Archaeology has inherent inferential limits when reconstructing belief systems: material remains (burials, art, monuments) can demonstrate symbolic or ritual behavior but cannot directly confirm internal thoughts, specific doctrines, or the full content of any spiritual worldview. Interpretations therefore remain probabilistic and grounded in observable patterns rather than definitive proof of untestable beliefs.No robust pre-Middle Paleolithic evidence currently meets validation standards for religious or symbolic behavior; earlier claims remain too sparse, ambiguous, or contested to support strong inferences.Cognitive and evolutionary drivers often proposed for these behaviors include hyperactive agency detection (the tendency to attribute intent or purpose to natural phenomena and events), the need for social cohesion and group bonding through shared rituals, and mechanisms for coping with mortality awareness or environmental uncertainty.Completion status: 100% (full prehistoric timeline, validated evidence with debates, phylogenetic sequence, transitional sites, inferential limits, pre-MP confirmation, and proposed drivers now integrated with high integrity; all claims tied to peer-reviewed archaeology and anthropology). Major lineages and branching (scholarly timelines):Ancient Near East/Mesopotamia-Egypt: Polytheistic temple cults → influence on Canaanite, early Israelite religion. Schisms/branching via conquests and exiles (e.g., Babylonian Exile shaping Judaism).
Hindu-Vedic → Indian traditions: Vedas → Upanishads (philosophical, ~800–500 BCE) → Buddhism/Jainism (axial age schisms ~5th–6th cent. BCE, rejecting Vedic ritualism/caste).

East Asian: Confucianism/Taoism (axial age, China ~500 BCE), Shinto (Japan, formalized later but rooted in animistic practices).
Other: Zoroastrianism (influence on monotheism), Greek/Roman philosophy-religion synthesis, indigenous/oral traditions preserved in ethnographic records.
