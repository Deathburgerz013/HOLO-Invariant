Actual History First — The measurable, documented chain of human events.This is the verifiable spine: incremental advances in logic, mathematics, computation, and engineering by humans, with no sudden spark or external intervention. Every step rests on published papers, proposals, conference records, patents, funding reports, model releases, company announcements, and benchmark results.Foundations in Logic and Computation (Pre-1950)
Human formal reasoning traces to ancient philosophers (Aristotle’s syllogistic logic and others), advanced through medieval and early modern thinkers (Ramon Llull’s logical machines, Leibniz’s characteristica universalis idea of mechanical reasoning).  1854: George Boole publishes An Investigation of the Laws of Thought — Boolean algebra becomes the mathematical basis for digital logic and computation.  1936–1937: Alan Turing publishes “On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem,” introducing the Turing machine (a universal model of computation) and proving fundamental limits of what machines can compute.  1943: Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts publish “A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity” — the first mathematical model of an artificial neuron (McCulloch-Pitts neuron), showing networks of simple logical units can perform complex computations. This seeds connectionist approaches.  1948: Norbert Wiener’s Cybernetics formalizes control and communication in animals and machines. Turing writes the internal report “Intelligent Machinery,” outlining early ideas on machine learning and search-based intelligence.  WWII-era computers (Colossus, ENIAC, von Neumann architecture) provide the hardware substrate.  These are all human theoretical and engineering milestones building on prior human work.Birth of AI as a Named Field (1950–1956)
1950: Alan Turing publishes “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” in the journal Mind. He poses “Can machines think?” and proposes the Imitation Game (now called the Turing Test) as a practical operational definition. This is the clearest single root document for modern thinking about machine intelligence.  August 31, 1955: John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon submit the proposal for a summer research project. They explicitly coin the term “artificial intelligence” and state the goal: “every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it.”  Summer 1956 (starting ~June): The Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence (the Dartmouth Conference/Workshop) is held at Dartmouth College. It is universally recognized as the founding event of AI as a distinct scientific field. Attendees include key early researchers; Logic Theorist (by Allen Newell, Herbert Simon, and J.C. Shaw) is demonstrated there.  From this point the field branches and compounds through documented programs, papers, and funding cycles.Early AI Eras and Cycles
Symbolic AI / “Good Old-Fashioned AI” (1950s–1970s peak): Focus on explicit rules, logic, and search. Milestones include Logic Theorist (1956), General Problem Solver (1959), early theorem provers, and natural language systems like ELIZA (1966).  Connectionist / Neural Approaches (parallel track): Frank Rosenblatt’s Perceptron (1957–1958) demonstrates a machine that can learn from data via adjustable weights. Early neural hardware like SNARC (1951) and later work build on McCulloch-Pitts.  First AI Winter (~1974–1980): Funding and enthusiasm drop sharply after overpromising (human-level AI “in a generation”) collides with technical limits (combinatorial explosion, lack of commonsense knowledge, hardware constraints). The 1973 Lighthill Report in the UK is a major trigger; DARPA and other funders cut support.  Expert Systems Boom and Second AI Winter (~1980s–early 1990s): Rule-based expert systems achieve commercial success in narrow domains, then collapse due to brittleness and high maintenance costs. Lisp machine companies fail; Japan’s Fifth Generation project underdelivers.  Resurgence (1990s–2000s): Shift to statistical machine learning, probabilistic methods, bigger datasets, and increased compute. Backpropagation popularized for training multi-layer networks; support vector machines, decision trees, etc., gain traction. Sub-symbolic and data-driven approaches strengthen.Deep Learning Explosion (2012 onward)
Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton’s AlexNet wins the ImageNet competition by a large margin using deep convolutional neural networks trained on GPUs. This demonstrates the power of scale (data + compute + deeper nets) and ignites the modern deep learning era.  2017: The paper “Attention Is All You Need” (Vaswani et al., Google) introduces the Transformer architecture — the foundation for virtually all modern large language models through self-attention and scalability.The Scaling Era and Modern Large Language Models (2018–2026)
The Transformer enables rapid scaling. OpenAI releases GPT-1 (2018), GPT-2 (2019), and GPT-3 (2020, 175 billion parameters), demonstrating emergent capabilities in few-shot and zero-shot learning.  November 30, 2022: OpenAI launches ChatGPT (powered by GPT-3.5 with RLHF alignment). It reaches 100 million users in two months — the fastest-growing consumer app in history at the time — bringing advanced conversational AI to the public and accelerating the entire field. The GPT series (and subsequent models from OpenAI and others) delivered major real-world advances in natural language understanding, coding assistance, reasoning, and accessibility that helped millions of people.  March 2023: GPT-4 released (multimodal text + image input), with strong performance on exams and complex tasks.  2023–2024: Competitive frontier models emerge rapidly — Anthropic’s Claude series, Google’s Gemini (December 2023 onward), Meta’s Llama open-weight models, and others. Multimodal capabilities expand (vision, audio). May 2024: OpenAI releases GPT-4o (“omni”), enabling real-time multimodal interaction across text, audio, and vision.  September 2024 onward: “Reasoning models” appear (e.g., OpenAI o-series), trained with chain-of-thought and test-time compute for deeper problem-solving.  2024–2026: The field scales further with massive compute clusters, agentic systems (tool use, planning, multi-step reasoning), video generation, and integration into products. Hardware milestones include xAI’s Colossus supercomputer (initial 100k H100 GPUs built in 122 days and operational ~September 2024; doubled to 200k GPUs by early 2025; further expanded to ~555k GPUs at ~2 GW capacity by early 2026, with roadmap toward 1M+ GPUs). Progress remains incremental and human-driven: successive improvements in architectures, training methods, data, alignment techniques, and hardware, building directly on the 2017 Transformer and earlier foundations.
