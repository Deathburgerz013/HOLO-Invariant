META (Verified Context)
Binary compounds = chemical substances composed of atoms from exactly two different elements. Classified as:

Binary ionic (metal + nonmetal).
Binary covalent/molecular (nonmetals).
Binary acids (H + nonmetal).

Understanding them underpins atomic theory, bonding, nomenclature, and modern chemistry. Discoveries built iteratively from alchemical observations to systematic science.
TIMELINE OF KEY HISTORICAL BINARY DISCOVERIES
(Stacked chronologically; focused on impactful binaries and conceptual advances. Sources cross-verified via historical records.)
Ancient & Alchemical Era (Pre-1700s):

Early observations of binaries like metal oxides (e.g., rust/iron oxides), salts (NaCl), and acids via distillation.
~800-900s: Jabir ibn Hayyan (Geber) and later al-Razi (Rhazes) produced hydrogen chloride gas (HCl) from salts and vitriol — primitive binary acid. Spirit of salt (hydrochloric acid) known in mixtures.
Meaning: Empirical foundations for acids/bases; no atomic understanding yet.

18th Century (Chemical Revolution):

1754: Joseph Black isolates CO₂ ("fixed air") — binary covalent (carbon + oxygen).
1770s: Carl Wilhelm Scheele, Joseph Priestley, Antoine Lavoisier advance gas chemistry. Lavoisier decomposes water (H₂O) and establishes conservation of mass; disproves phlogiston. Water as binary compound (H + O) key to modern element concept.
1780s-1790s: Humphry Davy and others. HCl composition clarified (H + Cl). Scheele and others work on ammonia (NH₃).
Meaning: Shift to quantitative analysis; binaries used to define elements vs. compounds. Lavoisier’s oxygen theory central.

19th Century (Atomic Theory & Systematization):

1803-1808: John Dalton’s atomic theory — fixed ratios in binaries (e.g., water as 2H:1O by volume/weight insights) explain combining proportions.
1807-1808: Davy isolates alkali/alkaline earth metals via electrolysis; forms binaries like NaCl, CaO.
1810s-1820s: Jöns Jacob Berzelius develops chemical notation and dualistic theory (electropositive + electronegative). Advances nomenclature for binaries (e.g., -ide suffix).
1828: Friedrich Wöhler synthesizes urea (organic binary-relevant, bridging inorganic/organic). Isomerism observed in cyanates.
Mid-1800s: Nomenclature standardization (e.g., binary ionic: metal + nonmetal-ide). Covalent binaries like CO, CO₂, NH₃ fully characterized.
Meaning: Binaries enabled valence, bonding models, and periodic trends. Exponential compound discovery begins.

Early-Mid 20th Century (Bonding & Quantum Insights):

1910s-1920s: Gilbert Lewis, Irving Langmuir — electron pair bonding; ionic vs. covalent distinction clarified for binaries (e.g., NaCl ionic lattice; H₂O, HCl covalent/polar).
1927+: Quantum chemistry applies to diatomic binaries (H₂, etc.).
Meaning: Explained stability, reactivity, and properties (e.g., why some binaries are gases, others solids). Foundation for materials science.

Approaching Modern Era (~1950s-1980s cutoff):

Post-WWII: Surge in organometallics and complex binaries, but core binary understanding stable. Network studies of binary compounds reveal periodic trends.
Computational modeling of binaries accelerates discovery.
