Below is a verified, evidence-based overview of the history of music, starting from its prehistoric origins. This draws from archaeological finds, historical records, and scholarly consensus. Music is a cultural universal, present in every known human society, though its exact "beginning" is unknowable—likely tied to early human (or pre-human) vocalizations, rhythms, and social behaviors. 

en.wikipedia.org

Prehistoric Origins (Before ~3000 BCE)Music almost certainly predates any written records. The human voice was likely the first "instrument"—used for singing, humming, chanting, and rhythmic sounds. Early percussion (clapping, stomping, striking objects) probably came next, possibly linked to labor, rituals, or communication. 

en.wikipedia.org

Key archaeological evidence:Bone flutes and whistles from the Upper Paleolithic (~40,000–38,000 years ago or earlier). Examples include flutes from German caves (e.g., Geissenklösterle) made from bird bones or mammoth ivory, and the disputed Divje Babe "flute" (a perforated cave bear femur, ~40,000+ years old, possibly Neanderthal). 

en.wikipedia.org

Instruments like bullroarers, rasps, and phalangeal whistles appear in Paleolithic sites.
Finds in China (Jiahu bone flutes, ~7000 BCE), Libya (~90,000–115,000-year-old bone fragment with a hole), Palestine (~10,000 BCE bird-bone flutes), and elsewhere show widespread early aerophones. 

en.wikipedia.org

In Africa, Egypt, Southern Africa, India (Indus Valley), Vietnam, and Australia, early idiophones, aerophones, and later stringed instruments (e.g., antler-based in Vietnam ~2,000+ years old) developed using local materials.

Theories on why music emerged (none universally proven):Sexual selection/mating displays (Darwin and others).
Byproduct of language evolution or a shared precursor ("musilanguage").
Social cohesion, labor coordination, ritual/shamanism, mother-infant communication, or emotional expression. 

en.wikipedia.org

Prehistoric music was likely monophonic (single melody), improvisational, and integrated with dance, ritual, and daily life. No notation survives—only instruments and contextual clues.


en.wikipedia.org

Mesopotamia/Egypt/India/China: Music served gods and rulers. Egyptian murals show flutes, harps, and percussion in rituals. Indian Vedas reference music; Chinese developed sophisticated tuning and instruments. Indus Valley yields early flutes and strings.
Hurrian Hymns (c. 1400 BCE, Ugarit/Syria): The oldest known notated music—clay tablets with cuneiform lyrics and notation for hymns (e.g., to goddess Nikkal). Reconstructions exist (search "Hurrian Hymn No. 6" on YouTube for playable versions using lyres or similar).

The Seikilos Epitaph (1st–2nd century CE, inscribed on a tombstone in Turkey) is the oldest complete surviving composition with melody and lyrics: a short, philosophical song about enjoying life.

This builds directly on your text for continuity.Ancient Music (~3000 BCE onward)With the rise of writing and complex civilizations, music becomes documented through artifacts, texts, and iconography. It remained predominantly monophonic (single melodic line), closely tied to religion, poetry, ritual, court ceremonies, and storytelling. Improvisation was central, and music often integrated with dance, drama, and recitation. Tuning systems, scales, and instruments evolved regionally using local materials (bone, wood, metal, gourds, shells). 

en.wikipedia.org

Key Civilizations and Evidence:Mesopotamia (Sumer, Babylon, etc.): Music served temples and palaces. Clay tablets describe instruments like lyres, harps, drums, and flutes. The Epic of Gilgamesh and other texts reference musicians. Royal tombs at Ur (c. 2500 BCE) yielded ornate lyres and bull-headed harps.
Ancient Egypt: Extensive iconography (murals, statues) depicts ensembles with harps, flutes, double clarinets, lutes, percussion, and singers in rituals and banquets. Music linked to gods like Hathor and Bes. Predynastic evidence includes clappers and early aerophones.
India: The Vedas (c. 1500–500 BCE) describe chanting (sāman) and instruments. Indus Valley Civilization (c. 3300–1300 BCE) yields early flutes and possible stringed instruments. Later, Natyashastra (c. 200 BCE–200 CE) formalized theory, rāgas, and performance.
China: Sophisticated court music (yayue) with bells, chimes, flutes, and zithers (e.g., guqin). The Book of Songs (Shijing, compiled c. 600 BCE) preserves lyrics. Jiahu bone flutes (~7000–5700 BCE, predating "ancient" but foundational) show early pentatonic scales. 

en.wikipedia.org

Other: Hebrew Psalms for temple worship; early Persian and East Asian traditions.

Oldest Notated Music: The Hurrian Hymns (c. 1400 BCE, Ugarit, modern Syria) — clay tablets with lyrics (to goddess Nikkal) and cuneiform notation indicating intervals for a lyre/harp. Reconstructions (e.g., Hymn No. 6) are playable and demonstrate diatonic-like elements. 

en.wikipedia.org

Ancient Greece and Rome (c. 800 BCE–500 CE)Greek philosophy profoundly shaped Western music theory: modes (e.g., Dorian, Phrygian), the doctrine of ethos (music's moral/ emotional influence on character), mathematics of intervals (Pythagoras), and integration with poetry/drama. Music was performed on lyres (kithara, barbitos), auloi (double-reed pipes), percussion, and in choruses for theater (tragedy/comedy) and Olympics. 

en.wikipedia.org

Seikilos Epitaph (1st–2nd century CE, Turkey): Oldest complete surviving composition with melody and lyrics — a short, stoic song inscribed on a tombstone. Notation uses Greek letters. 

en.wikipedia.org

Rome adopted and spread Greek practices, adding brass (e.g., tuba) for military use. Early Christian music drew from Jewish and Greco-Roman traditions, leading into medieval chant.

Instruments proliferated: lyres, harps, lutes, various flutes, drums, early hydraulis (water organ).Medieval Period (c. 500–1400 CE)Western Europe: Early Christian liturgy emphasized monophonic plainchant (Gregorian chant under Pope Gregory I, though evolved earlier). Modal system, Latin texts, unaccompanied for worship. Polyphony emerged (9th–13th centuries): organum (parallel voices), then complex forms at Notre Dame School (Léonin, Pérotin — measured rhythm, multiple independent lines). Neumatic and staff notation (Guido d'Arezzo) enabled preservation and complexity.

Secular: Troubadours/trouvères (courtly love songs in vernacular), minnesingers, jongleurs. Folk traditions varied widely.Islamic Golden Age (8th–13th centuries): Advanced theory (al-Farabi, Avicenna — scales, modes, influence on Europe via Al-Andalus/Spain). Rich traditions in maqām, oud, percussion. Persian, Arabic, and North African influences.Global: African polyrhythms and vocal traditions; East Asian court/orchestral music; Indigenous Americas with flutes, drums, rattles tied to ceremony; Indian rāga systems matured; Chinese/ Japanese gagaku and nō.Renaissance (c. 1400–1600)Humanism emphasized text expression, clearer polyphony, and balance. Peak composers: Josquin des Prez, Palestrina (masses/motets for clarity post-Council of Trent). Secular forms: madrigals (word-painting), chansons, lute songs. Instrumental music grew (viols, recorders, lutes, early keyboards). Music printing (Petrucci, 1501) revolutionized dissemination.

Baroque (c. 1600–1750)Contrast, drama, ornamentation, and emotional expressiveness. Opera invented (Peri, Monteverdi — L'Orfeo, 1607). Basso continuo, tonal harmony solidifies. Virtuosity rises. Key figures: J.S. Bach (fugues, cantatas), Handel (oratorios, Messiah), Vivaldi (Four Seasons), Purcell. Instruments: violin family matures, harpsichord, organ. Public concerts emerge.

Classical (c. 1750–1820)Clarity, symmetry, formal structures (sonata-allegro, symphony, concerto, string quartet). "Viennese School": Haydn ("Father of the Symphony"), Mozart (operas, symphonies), early Beethoven. Orchestra standardizes; public concerts democratize access; fortepiano rises. Enlightenment ideals of balance and reason.
