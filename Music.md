Ancient Music (~3000 BCE onward)With writing and civilizations, music becomes more documented. It was often monophonic, tied to religion, poetry, and court life, with improvisation common. 

en.wikipedia.org

Mesopotamia/Egypt/India/China: Music served gods and rulers. Egyptian murals show flutes, harps, and percussion in rituals. Indian Vedas reference music; Chinese developed sophisticated tuning and instruments. Indus Valley yields early flutes and strings.
Hurrian Hymns (c. 1400 BCE, Ugarit/Syria): The oldest known notated music—clay tablets with cuneiform lyrics and notation for hymns (e.g., to goddess Nikkal). Reconstructions exist (search "Hurrian Hymn No. 6" on YouTube for playable versions using lyres or similar).

Ancient Greece (and later Rome): Profound influence on Western theory (modes, ethos—music's emotional/moral power). The Seikilos Epitaph (1st–2nd century CE, inscribed on a tombstone in Turkey) is the oldest complete surviving composition with melody and lyrics: a short, philosophical song about enjoying life. 

en.wikipedia.org

Other traditions: Persian, Hebrew (Psalms), and early East Asian court music.

Instruments evolved: lyres, harps, lutes, drums, flutes, and early organs.Medieval Period (~500–1400 CE)Western classical tradition begins with early Christian Church music (plainchant/Gregorian chant—monophonic, Latin, for liturgy). Polyphony (multiple independent voices) develops later (e.g., Notre Dame school, 12th–13th centuries). Staff notation emerges, allowing preservation.

Secular music (troubadours, trouvères, minnesingers) and folk traditions flourished alongside. Islamic Golden Age advanced music theory, influencing Europe via Spain. African, Asian, and Indigenous American traditions continued independently with rich vocal, percussive, and instrumental forms.Renaissance (c. 1400–1600)Polyphony peaks (e.g., Palestrina, Josquin). Humanism brings more expressive, text-driven music. Printing (e.g., Ottaviano Petrucci) spreads sheet music. Madrigals, masses, and early instrumental music (viols, lutes) grow.

Baroque (c. 1600–1750)Drama, ornamentation, and contrast dominate. Opera emerges (Monteverdi). Key figures: Bach, Handel, Vivaldi. Basso continuo, tonality, and virtuoso instruments (violin, harpsichord).

Classical (c. 1750–1820)Clarity, balance, and form (sonata, symphony). Haydn, Mozart, early Beethoven. Public concerts rise; orchestra standardizes.

Romantic (c. 1820–1900)Emotion, nationalism, program music. Beethoven (late), Chopin, Wagner, Tchaikovsky, etc. Larger orchestras, virtuoso soloists, expanded harmony.20th–21st CenturiesModernism/Experimental: Atonality, serialism (Schoenberg), minimalism (Reich, Glass).
Jazz, Blues, Rock, Pop, Hip-Hop, Electronic: Explosion of popular genres via recording tech (Edison cylinder → vinyl → digital). African-American innovations heavily shape global music.
Global fusion: Recording, radio, internet, and globalization mix traditions (e.g., Bollywood, K-pop, Afrobeats).
Technology: Synthesizers, sampling, AI-generated music today.

Music has always evolved through cultural exchange (e.g., Silk Road), technology, and social change. Popular music now dominates dissemination, but "art music," folk, and traditional forms persist worldwide.
